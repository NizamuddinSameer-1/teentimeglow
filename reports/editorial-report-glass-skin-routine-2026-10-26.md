## 2. AI-tell detection

### 2.1 The single worst tell: the negation-then-correction engine

This is the diagnosis. Not the punchy closers — those are downstream of it.

The article's sentence-level engine is: **assert what the thing is not, then assert what it is.** Every section does it. I found eight clean `not X. It is Y.` pairs and at least six looser variants — roughly **fourteen negation-correction moves in 1,887 words, one every 135 words.**

Clean pairs:

1. "It describes a finish, not a skin type."
2. "Nine steps is not a method. It is a list of things to buy, arranged so each feels necessary."
3. "Fewer steps is not the busy-girl compromise. It is the correct answer for the skin you have."
4. `"Then stop" is not a joke about self-control. It is the technique.`
5. "AHA and BHA exfoliants are not evil. They are easy to overuse…"
6. "A glass skin routine for oily or acne-prone skin is not a different routine. It is this one with two swaps."
7. "What decides it is not which products you chose. It is whether you did it on the ordinary mornings."
8. "This is general information, not medical advice."

Looser variants: "not a shortcut," "real, not aspirational," "Not three.", "Not to chase a stronger ingredient.", "plump rather than shiny, even rather than pale" (a double), "not from any single product," "Not without a doctor involved."

**Why this is worse than the punchy closers.** The reversal is a good move used well maybe three times. Used fourteen times it becomes a *stance*: the article is in permanent argument with a reader who never speaks. For a 15-year-old who arrived already defensive about her skin (the brief says this explicitly in §3, point 5), being corrected fourteen times in nine minutes is exhausting even when every correction is right. It is the difference between "here's how this works" and "here's what you've got wrong."

It also produces the punchy closers mechanically. Once you've denied a misconception, the assertion that follows *wants* to be short — that's how the reversal resolves. So the drumbeat at the end of every section is a symptom of the engine, not a separate habit.

**Fix:** cut roughly half the reversals, and where you keep one, let the assertion run long instead of short. I've marked which in §6.

### 2.2 The punchy-closer habit: voice or tic?

**Tic — but a fixable one, because five of them are genuinely good.** Here is the section-by-section audit. Eight of nine sections end on a short declarative.

| Section | Closer | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| H2 #1 | "Not to chase a stronger ingredient." | **Cut.** The previous sentence already said the routine adds water, seals it, protects it. This is the negation of an idea the reader was never going to have. |
| H2 #3 | "A misplaced step does not only underperform; it can stop the step before it from working." | **Keep the idea, cut the flourish.** The semicolon + "does not only" is the most writerly-bothered sentence in the piece. |
| H2 #4 | "Add it up: 45 plus 50 plus 45 plus 100 is 240 seconds. Four minutes, exactly." | **Keep the beat, cut one word.** See below. |
| H2 #5 | "Three minutes cleansing, two treating, one sealing. Six in total, and then you are finished." | **Cut the last clause.** The reader has just been told to leave the bathroom. "And then you are finished" explains the exit they already took. |
| H2 #6 | "A pharmacist can answer a label question faster than the internet can." | **Keep.** It's specific, it's useful, and it's the one closer that gives her somewhere to go. |
| H2 #7 | "That is a doctor conversation rather than a product swap." | **Keep.** Correct register at the correct moment. |
| H2 #8 | "None of those are failures. They are past what a routine can do." | **Keep — best closer in the article.** It is the only one that protects the reader instead of performing for her. |
| Closing | "That is the whole thesis." | **Cut, unconditionally.** See §7. |

So: **keep four, cut four.** And note the pattern — the ones that work are the ones carrying *information the reader needs* (where to go, what it means, you are not failing). The ones that fail are the ones carrying *the writer's satisfaction at having said something neat* ("Not to chase a stronger ingredient", "That is the whole thesis", "That number is the point").

**"Four minutes, exactly."** — the beat is earned; the reader just did the arithmetic with you, and the total is the payoff. But `exactly` is the smug word. "Four minutes." is a receipt. "Four minutes, exactly." is a magician's flourish. Cut `exactly`.

**"Then stop"** — this is the exception that proves the rule, and I'd keep it in both places (the H3 title and the quoted phrase in the body). It is the article's one memorable phrase, it is behavioural rather than abstract, and the brief is right that it is load-bearing. My problem with that section is not the phrase — it's that the phrase is doing work the explanation should be doing. See §8.

### 2.3 The "That is / It is / This is" subject habit

Measured: **4 × "That is…", 9 × "It is…", 4 × "This is…"** as sentence openers — 17 demonstrative-subject sentences out of 155, about 11%. Roughly every fourth sentence points backwards at the previous one.

Individually these are fine. Cumulatively they make the prose feel like it is narrating itself. Worst cluster is H2 #6–#7, where four consecutive paragraph units open on a demonstrative or a fragment: "This is the part I most want you to read" / "Two smaller warnings." / "This is general information, not medical advice." / "A glass skin routine for oily or acne-prone skin is not a different routine." / "That is the first swap."

**Fix:** in §6 I break up four of these by opening on the *object* instead of the *point* — "The bottle goes down," "Two things go wrong here," "Your morning rinse."

### 2.4 Rhetorical-question openers

Only four, and two are keyword-mandated ("Do teens need retinol?", "How long does it take to get glass skin?"). That's fine and I'm not calling it.

The two that aren't:

- **"Not sure which version fits you?"** — this is the marketer's pivot question, and it's the one place in the article where the voice audibly changes from Rini to a landing page. Rewrite as a statement: *"If you're not sure which version is yours, the quiz takes two minutes."*
- **"New product?"** — acceptable as a fragment, but it's the fourth fragment-question in a row in that stretch. Merge it into the sentence: *"If it's a new product, patch test it on your inner forearm or along your jawline and leave it 24 hours before it goes anywhere near your whole face."*

### 2.5 Tidy symmetrical paragraph lengths

This is measurable and it is the thing a reader feels without being able to name. Sixty-nine prose units. Word counts: 19, 52, 33, 43, 46, 22, 24, 30, 40, 19, 33, 22, 48, 20, 28, 37, 35, 33, 33, 24, 31…

- **No paragraph is longer than 63 words.**
- **Exactly one paragraph is a single sentence** (and it's a bullet list artefact).
- **Not one paragraph runs past four sentences.**

Real writing has a five-word paragraph and then an eighty-word one, because a thought occasionally arrives whole. Here every thought has been pre-cut to the same length. That is the strongest structural signal in the draft, and it survives all the way through — including in the sections that should breathe most (the barrier explanation, the retinol argument).

**Fix:** merge three pairs of short paragraphs into 5–6 sentence runs (I mark them in §6), and let one paragraph be three words. The piece needs about four places where the rhythm breaks.

### 2.6 "Two" as a structural crutch

Eight instances of "Two": *The Two Things That Create The Look* / *The two steps to skip* / *Change These Two Things* / *Two smaller warnings* / "two swaps" / "two minutes" / "two or three nights" / "a minute or two."

The "two things" framing is doing structural work it hasn't earned — it's a way of making a list feel considered. Twice is a device. Five times is a verbal habit. **Cut "Two smaller warnings."** and let the paragraph stand; drop "two swaps" in H2 #7 (see §6, edit 11 — it's also factually off).

### 2.7 Signposting / scaffolding sentences

Four sentences exist to tell the reader where they are rather than to tell them anything:

- "Here is what I can give you: the real method, in order, with a clock on it."
- "The rest of this is one routine, fixed order, real timings, and a short list of what to hold."
- "This is the part I most want you to read, which is why it sits after the routine and not before it."
- "Two smaller warnings."

**Keep the third one.** It's the single most skilful sentence in the article — it manages the reader's defensiveness before the safety material starts, which is the exact failure mode the brief warns about in §8.3. Cut or compress the other three.

## 3. The keyword sentences

This is where the draft is closest to unsalvageable, and it is the cheapest fix in the report. Four exact-match phrases have been inserted as *labels* rather than as *claims* — the sentence states the keyword and then stops, which is why they read as pasted.

The diagnostic is simple: **every one of these sentences uses "is" where a person would use a verb.** "is not a different routine", "is three steps", "is actually written", "it is four steps because four is". Copula after copula. A person writing about a routine says what the routine *does*.

I've given each a rewrite that keeps the exact phrase where it earns its place and drops it where the H1 or H2 already carries the coverage.

### 3.1 "It is a bad hour to be looking up a korean glass skin routine."

**Problem:** two. First, `korean` is lowercase mid-sentence — that alone marks the sentence as keyword-inserted, and a sharp 15-year-old will clock it as a typo or as SEO. Second, "looking up a korean glass skin routine" is not how anyone phrases a search; she'd say "looking up glass skin."

**Rewrite (recommended):** *"It is a bad hour to be googling glass skin."*

**Trade:** loses the exact match. I recommend taking the loss — the exact phrase already appears in the locked H1 and in the slug, so this instance is pure redundancy, and it is the *first keyword sentence the reader meets*, sitting in paragraph two. First impressions of a keyword are worth more than a repeat.

**If the exact match must stay:** *"It is a bad hour to be reading about a Korean glass skin routine."* — one capital letter, one verb change, and it stops reading as a paste.

### 3.2 "Most glass skin routine for teens advice gets closed in a tab before the bus."

**Problem:** this is not English. You cannot premodify "advice" with a four-word keyword phrase; the noun stack collapses. This is the single worst sentence in the draft and the one most likely to cost you the reader's trust in paragraph two.

**Rewrite:** *"Most of it gets closed in a tab before the bus."*

The antecedent is already established by the previous sentence ("Almost everything you will find…"), so "Most of it" is tighter and it keeps the good half of the sentence (the tab, the bus).

### 3.3 "Almost no glass skin routine for teens is actually written for a teenager."

**Problem:** three small ones that compound. `actually` is a hedge that adds nothing. "for teens… for a teenager" echoes in a way that sounds like a word-count problem. And the sentence is the first half of a reversal ("It is written by a shop, for a shopper…") that we're trying to reduce the supply of.

**Rewrite — merge both sentences and end on the accusation:**

> *"Almost every glass skin routine for teens is written by a shop, for a shopper, and the shopper is assumed to be twenty-eight with a card. Nothing on that page was addressed to you."*

**Trade:** keeps the exact match, kills `actually`, kills one reversal, and converts a two-sentence explanation into one sentence plus a line that lands. This is the exact phrase's best home in the article — keep this one and let the lede instance go.

### 3.4 "This is the glass skin routine for school mornings, and it is four steps because four is what fits between waking up and leaving."

**Problem:** 24 words, and the logic is circular — "four steps because four is what fits" says nothing twice. Also "This is the glass skin routine for school mornings" is a caption, not a sentence.

**Rewrite (recommended, minimal):** *"This is the glass skin routine for school mornings: four steps, because four is what fits between waking up and leaving."*

A colon instead of "and it is" fixes most of the strain at the cost of one character. **This is the one exact match I would not give up** — it is the article's differentiator, the H2 is only a partial match ("The School Morning Routine: Four Steps, Four Minutes"), and no other sentence in the piece carries "school mornings" plus "glass skin routine" together.

**Stronger, if you'll take the risk:** *"Four steps is what fits between waking up and leaving, so this is the glass skin routine for school mornings and nothing else."* — but that's 26 words and it buries the phrase. I'd take the colon.

### 3.5 "A glass skin routine for oily or acne-prone skin is not a different routine. It is this one with two swaps."

**Problem:** the reversal, plus "It is this one" is circular, plus **"two swaps" is factually off** — see §6, edit 11. The section promises two changes and delivers two *cleanser* adjustments, which the H3 then calls "Change The Cleanser, Not The Routine" (singular). The reader who counts will notice.

**Rewrite:** *"A glass skin routine for oily or acne-prone skin is the same routine with two swaps in it, and both of them are in the cleanser."*

Keeps the exact match, one sentence instead of two, kills the reversal, and makes "two" true.

### 3.6 "The glass skin routine at night is three steps."

**Problem:** the flattest sentence in the article. Pure copula, zero information beyond the heading directly above it, and it's sitting inside a paragraph that has a much better fact available (morning four + night three = the seven the quiz promises).

**Rewrite:** *"The glass skin routine at night drops to three steps."*

One verb — `drops` — and the sentence now carries a fact (there is a reduction, and the reduction is the system) instead of restating the H2. Keeps the exact match at the front. **This is the highest-value single-word edit in the report.**

### 3.7 Verdict on exact-match coverage

My editorial recommendation, for the lead to trade against the auditor:

| Sentence | Exact match | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| 3.1 lede | `korean glass skin routine` | **Drop.** Covered by H1 + slug. Replace with natural variant. |
| 3.2 lede | `glass skin routine for teens` | **Drop.** The sentence is broken; it cannot be saved with the phrase in it. |
| 3.3 H2 #2 body | `glass skin routine for teens` | **Keep.** Rewrite as given. This is the phrase's real home. |
| 3.4 H2 #4 body | `glass skin routine for school mornings` | **Keep.** Colon fix only. |
| 3.5 H2 #7 body | `glass skin routine for oily` | **Keep.** Rewrite as given. |
| 3.6 H2 #5 body | `glass skin routine at night` | **Keep.** One verb change. |

Net: four exact matches retained, two surrendered, six sentences that read like prose. That is a better trade than six retained and two broken sentences, and I'd defend it on the grounds that a strained keyword sentence damages the whole page's credibility while a missing long-tail repeat costs one occurrence of a phrase the H1 already owns.

**The problem is not the level, it is the variance.** A grade-5 article is not automatically condescending — but a grade-5 article where 39% of sentences are under nine words and the median is ten has exactly one gear. There is no sentence in 1,887 words that asks the reader to hold two clauses in her head. That is why it reads like a slide deck: not because it's simple, but because it never varies.

Raising the level is not the fix. **Raising the variance is.** Add four long sentences (25–35 words) at the points where the argument is actually complex — the barrier explanation, the retinol reasoning, the purging distinction — and the average will barely move while the piece stops sounding metronomic.

### Longest sentences

| Words | Sentence | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| 52* | *(bullet list artefact — five bullets parsed as one sentence)* | Not real. Ignore. |
| 25 | "Glass skin is a Korean beauty term for skin that looks clear, smooth, and lit from within: plump rather than shiny, even rather than pale." | Fine. Colon does the work. This is the model for the longer sentences I want more of. |
| 25 | "When the top layers hold water, the surface flattens, light reflects in a straighter line, and the result reads as glass rather than as shine." | Fine — three-clause build, correctly ordered. |
| 25 | "Hold this one until you are older, and if a dermatologist suggests it for a specific reason, that is their call and not a blog's." | Good sentence, wrong first clause (see §9). |

| 24 | "Skin that gets a third layer at ten at night because it looked dry is being asked to absorb something it did not need." | **Needs two passes.** See below. |

### Sentences that need two passes

1. **"Skin that gets a third layer at ten at night because it looked dry is being asked to absorb something it did not need."**
   Three problems. The subject is nine words long before the verb arrives, so the reader is garden-pathed. `because it looked dry` is ambiguous — does "it" mean the skin or the layer? And "is being asked to absorb something" is passive *and* anthropomorphic; skin doesn't get asked. This is the sentence carrying the whole "then stop" argument and it is the hardest sentence in the piece. Rewrite in §6, edit 7.

2. **"A misplaced step does not only underperform; it can stop the step before it from working."**
   "does not only underperform" is a construction nobody says out loud, and "the step before it from working" needs a re-read. Rewrite: *"A misplaced step doesn't just underperform. It can switch off the step before it."*

3. **"Her skin is not your skin, and a 13-year-old's routine is not a 17-year-old's."**
   Two unrelated points welded with "and". The first is about borrowing a friend's product; the second is about age. Split them, or drop the second.

4. **"What decides it is not which products you chose. It is whether you did it on the ordinary mornings."**
   "What decides it" has no referent — decides *what*? The reader has to go back a paragraph. And it's the reversal again. Rewrite: *"The products barely matter next to this: whether you did it on the ordinary mornings."*

5. **"This takes longer than the morning because you are removing a full day of sunscreen, sweat, and oil."**
   Not hard to read, but "a full day of sunscreen, sweat, and oil" is a tricolon that could be two items and would be better as two. Minor.

### The passage that reads most like an adult

Flagged here, judged in §9: **"Yours has not."**

## 7. Verdict on the ending

**Does it earn the callback?** The 6:40 mirror — yes, just about. It's opened in the first sentence, returned to in the last paragraph, and it's a real frame rather than a bolted-on one. But **the callback that actually works is not the mirror. It's "week three."** "A routine that only grows is a routine you quit by week three" appears in H2 #2, and "the perfect one stops in week three" lands in the final line. That's a 1,700-word callback to a specific detail, and it's the best structural thing in the article. The mirror is decoration; week three is the argument closing.

**Is it too neat?** Two problems, both fixable in one line each.

First, **"That is the whole thesis."** — see edit 13. It's a stage direction. Cut it and the sentence after it becomes the last word, which is where it belongs.

Second, the closing paragraph **piles up three CTAs after the thesis has landed**: start with the morning four / the skincare hub / the wallpaper. The first is a real instruction and should stay. ## 8. Judgement call: is "Then stop" doing enough work?

**No. It is a good line standing in for an explanation, and it's the article's most important section, which makes this the most expensive weakness in the draft.**

Here's the diagnosis. The section contains four sentences:

> "Same moisturizer as the morning. Press it over everything, including the treatment, then put the bottle down and leave the bathroom."
> `"Then stop" is not a joke about self-control. It is the technique. Skin left alone overnight can do the repair work. Skin that gets a third layer at ten at night because it looked dry is being asked to absorb something it did not need.`

The first sentence is **excellent** — "put the bottle down and leave the bathroom" is behavioural, physical, and impossible to misread. That's what technique-writing looks like.

Then the explanation does three things and none of them is the actual mechanism:
- It **names** the technique ("It is the technique") instead of describing it.
- It makes a **vague vitalist claim** ("skin left alone overnight can do the repair work") — true in spirit, but a 15-year-old cannot act on it.
- It **describes the failure** ("a third layer at ten at night because it looked dry") without ever naming the sensation that causes it.

The missing piece is the thing that would make this section the best thing in the article: **the tight feeling ten minutes after moisturizer is the product drying down, not your skin asking for more.** That single fact explains the behaviour (why she reaches for the third layer), reframes the sensation (the cue is lying to her), and makes "then stop" a technique rather than a personality trait. Right now the reader is being asked to exercise discipline against an urge the article never explains. That's why it reads as a nice line — because it's asking her to take the writer's word for it.

I've written the replacement in §6, edit 7. It adds about 45 words. Take them from H2 #2 and H2 #6, which the brief already says are 60 words over budget each.

**One more thing:** this section is the article's thesis in miniature. H2 #2 argues "fewer steps, because fewer is correct." Step 7 is where that argument becomes a behaviour at 10pm on a Tuesday. It deserves the most careful paragraph in the piece and currently has the least explained one.

## 9. Judgement call: is the retinol framing working, or is it a finger wag?

**Mostly working — and the reason it works is not the retinol passage. It's the section opener, which is the best paragraph in the article.**

> "This is the part I most want you to read, which is why it sits after the routine and not before it. Nothing here criticizes what you are already doing. It is a hold list."

That is genuinely skilled. It does four things in three sentences: it flags importance without alarm, it justifies the placement, it disarms the reader's defensiveness *before* the content arrives (which is precisely the failure mode the brief's §8.3 warns about), and it renames the section's job as "hold" rather than "don't." Keep it verbatim. It is also the sentence I'd point to as the template for the site's house voice.

**But the retinol passage underneath it does wag a finger, twice, and both times it's a word choice rather than an idea.**

> "Do teens need retinol? Not without a doctor involved, and **that is the honest answer almost no page will give you.**"

**First wag: "the honest answer almost no page will give you."** This is the article patting itself on the back at the exact moment it's asking the reader to trust it. It also claims something unverifiable — that no other page says this — and the brief's §4.1 shows at least one competitor (skincarelab.uk) does gesture at restraint. A shop claiming to be the only honest voice is still a shop claiming something. Cut it; the answer's plainness is the credential, and announcing the credential spends it.

> "Retinol is a strong active built around skin that has stopped renewing as quickly. **Yours has not.**"

**Second wag: "Yours has not."** Two words, full stop, and it is the single most adult-in-the-room moment in the article. It's not the content — the content is correct — it's the *grammar of dismissal*. A clipped sentence after a statement about her body reads as a verdict about her, and it arrives one line after the piece promised "nothing here criticizes what you are already doing." That's the one place the article breaks its own promise.

The fix is not to soften the advice. It's to **give her the mechanism instead of the verdict**: "Yours is still doing that on its own, which is exactly why you don't need the help." Same information, same firmness, and it treats her as someone who can be told how her skin works rather than someone who needs to be told she's too young.

**Third, smaller: "Hold this one until you are older."** "Older" is a verdict about her age, which is the one thing she can't act on. Tie the hold to a skin condition instead — *"until your skin has stopped changing on its own"* — which is both more accurate and gives her something to understand rather than something to wait out.

**Overall verdict:** the framing is right and the placement is right (after the routine, as the brief demands). Two sentences are doing the finger-wagging and both are replaceable without weakening the advice. With edit 8 applied, this section becomes the most valuable 280 words on the page — it's the only part of the article that says something a shop structurally cannot say.

## 15. Amendment — US-English residue, and one of them is in the safety list

*Added after `link-strategist` flagged that "getting you down" reads British. They were right, so I ran a full BrE lexicon pass rather than patching the one instance. The scan found a worse one they hadn't seen, in the escalation list.*

### 15.1 What the scan found

**The spelling layer is clean.** I checked for every common BrE spelling variant — `-our`, `-ise`, `-re`, `grey`, `travelled`, `labelled`, `practise`, `defence`, `whilst`, `towards`, `learnt` — and found zero. The draft has `gray`, `labeled`, `moisturizer`, `recognize`-class forms all correct. The writer was disciplined here.

**The lexical layer is not clean.** British-English *words* are all spelled correctly, which is exactly why a spellcheck will never catch them. Two remain:

| # | Line | Text | Problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 205 | "or **spots** that leave pits or marks" | **In US English, "spot" is a mark or a stain, not a pimple.** A US reader parses this as *"marks that leave pits or marks"* — circular, and in a safety criterion. |
| 2 | 185 | "A breakout is new **spots** in new places" | Same substitution. Also "new spots in new places" repeats "new" to no effect. |
| 3 | 203–211 | *(the H3 and list as a whole)* | Flagged by link-strategist: "getting you down" is BrE idiom. See §15.3. |

### 15.2 The safety bullet — highest-consequence item in this amendment

Line 205 currently reads:

> - You have painful or cystic acne, or **spots** that leave pits or marks

A British 15-year-old reads *"pimples that leave marks."* An American 15-year-old reads *"marks that leave pits or marks"* and gets nothing. This is the criterion that is supposed to route a reader with scarring acne to a doctor, and for the actual target audience it is currently unparseable. It is also the third time in six lines that the list reaches for scar-and-mark vocabulary, so the list is simultaneously ambiguous and repetitive.

**Proposed:**

> - You have painful or cystic acne, or **pimples that are leaving scars**

And line 209, grammar fix only, stays as the catch-all for non-acne causes:

> - **Anything that is scarring**

`pimples` rather than `zits` here deliberately — in a safety list the word has to be unambiguous to every reader in the range, including a 13-year-old and a 19-year-old from different regions. `zits` is available for voice elsewhere if the lead wants it; it is not available in a diagnostic criterion.

### 15.3 Line 185 — the purging paragraph

> "Purging is a short, predictable reaction in the places you normally break out, and it settles. A breakout is new spots in new places, and it gets worse rather than better."

**Proposed:**

> "Purging is a short, predictable reaction in the places you normally break out, and it settles. A breakout means pimples in places you don't normally get them, and it gets worse rather than better."

This also fixes the "new…new" echo, and "places you don't normally get them" is a clearer statement of the actual distinction than "new places" was.

### 15.4 Line 203 — accepting link-strategist's idiom fix

They're right, and I should have caught it: **"getting you down" is BrE.** American teens say *bringing you down* (or *bumming you out*, which is too casual for a safety list).

**Final wording for the §14.2 bullet:**

> - **Thinking about your skin is bringing you down most days**

Ten words, parallel with the other clauses, US idiom, no clinical vocabulary, no reversal. This supersedes the wording in §14.2 — apply this version.

### 15.5 Process note, for article two

A US-English instruction gets implemented as a *spelling* instruction and then verified with a spellcheck, which is structurally incapable of catching this class of error: `spot`, `getting you down`, `quite`, `trainers`, `revision` and `year 10` are all correctly spelled British English.

The check that works is a **lexicon scan against a fixed list**, run once per article, after the copy is final. It takes a minute. I've run it here and the only residue is the three items above, but the house style section (§12) should carry the rule so it doesn't depend on someone remembering:

> **Language:** US English throughout — spelling *and* vocabulary. Run a BrE lexicon scan before publish, not a spellcheck. Watch specifically: spot/pimple, get-down/bring-down, quite, mum, autumn/fall, trainers, revision, year N vs Nth grade.

### 16.2 Three objective errors that survived integration

These are not judgement calls. They are a typo, a dialect error, and a broken sentence, and all three are in a file marked FINAL.

**1. Line 33 — lowercase proper noun, in the first 50 words.**
> "It is a bad hour to be looking up a **korean** glass skin routine."

`korean` is still lowercase. This was the single most visible defect in my original report (§3.1) — it is the first keyword sentence the reader meets, and a capital letter is free. **Fix:** capital K, or take the natural variant *"It is a bad hour to be googling glass skin."*

**2. Line 193 — "GP".**
> "…what a pharmacist can help with and what needs a **GP**."

British. An American teenager has no GP. It also makes three names for one idea within forty lines: *dermatologist* (171), *GP* (193), *doctor* (209). Fix in §16.3.

**3. Line 214 — "Anything is scarring."**

Ungrammatical (a bullet with no subject-verb agreement that reads as a sentence fragment), and it now duplicates line 211, which says "pimples that are leaving scars." Two adjacent criteria for the same clinical outcome, one of them broken. **Fix:** delete 214 and widen 211 to **"You have painful or cystic acne, or anything that is leaving a scar."** That keeps the catch-all for non-acne scarring, fixes the grammar, and leaves five clean items with the mood bullet still last.

### 16.3 The line 193 replacement, verified against the source

link-strategist proposed replacing the NHS citation with the AAD. I fetched the AAD page myself before agreeing to attach my safety bullet to it, because that bullet is now load-bearing for a health claim read by minors. **Verified, with one correction to their wording:**

- **Scarring — supported, but they overstated it.** The page says *"Without treatment, acne sometimes becomes severe. When severe acne clears, it can leave permanent acne scars."* The condition is **severe** acne, not untreated acne generally. Write "severe acne can leave permanent scars," not "untreated acne can leave permanent scars."
- **Mood — strongly supported.** Point 4, *"Watch for signs of depression"*: *"Many studies have shown that having acne can lead to depression, anxiety, or both… it may be time to see a dermatologist for acne treatment."*
- **"Painful or deep" — not supported.** Keep the citation off that clause, as they said.

**One thing neither of us had noticed: the page is addressed to parents**, not teens — "your teenager," "if your teen seems upset," "be cautious about reminding your teen." Citing it to a 15-year-old as "guidance for teen acne" is a register mismatch. Naming that honestly turns it into an action:

> If what you have is painful, deep, or leaving scars, that is a doctor conversation rather than a product swap. The AAD's [advice for parents of teens with acne](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/acne/acne-teenage-five-ways-to-help) is worth showing to yours: severe acne can leave permanent scars, and they list the signs of depression as a reason to get treatment.

*"Worth showing to yours"* is the sentence I'd defend hardest in this whole report. It is honest about who the source was written for, it gives the reader something to actually do, and — the reason it matters — **it sources the mental-health bullet from the AAD instead of from Rini.** A blogger asserting "your mood counts" is worth less than a dermatology academy saying it. It also keeps the link out of the list.

**Drop the NHS link rather than replace it.** US audience, US keywords, UK health service. Line 187 already defines non-comedogenic inline, so nothing is left uncited.
